2 The basic idea of the course

Teaching language through literature

It means using a poem, story, novel or play to help learners improve English. The class may develop vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation and the four skills while learners understand and respond to the text. Literature is the material; language learning is the main purpose.

Example. A story says, "The boy crept towards the door." Students infer crept, compare it with walked and rushed, discuss why the boy moved quietly, then write a new sentence. One meaningful event produces reading, vocabulary, speaking and writing.

Analogy. A vocabulary list supplies bricks. A story shows how those bricks make a house. Learners see words working together, rather than meeting them in isolation.

Important distinction. Studying literature asks about themes, authors, movements and artistic effects. Using literature for language learning asks what learners can practise through the text. These aims can overlap. A good language lesson preserves the meaning of the story rather than reducing it to a grammar worksheet. Lazar makes this distinction in Chapter 1; Collie and Slater connect meaningful reading with learner involvement.

Essential terms before you continue

Linguistic competence is knowledge of vocabulary and language patterns. Communicative competence includes using that knowledge appropriately in interaction. Literary competence includes knowing how to interpret a narrator, metaphor, irony or other literary conventions. A learner can understand every word and still miss the irony. Lazar discusses this separation in Chapters 1 and 3.

Scaffolding is temporary help that allows learners to do something they cannot yet do alone: a glossary, picture, short background explanation or guiding question. Exploitability means the teaching possibilities a text offers. A short scene with requests and refusals is exploitable for speaking and politeness.

Intensive reading examines a short passage closely. Extensive reading involves reading larger amounts for general understanding and enjoyment. Both can help: close reading explores a particular word; extensive reading meets words repeatedly across a book. Daskalovska and Dimova connect literature with extensive reading and learner independence.

3 Literary language and everyday language

Is literary language a separate language

There is no separate English grammar used only in literature. Literary and everyday language share words, structures and devices. Literature often uses these resources with greater concentration or artistic purpose. The strongest position is therefore a continuum of literariness, rather than a hard border.

Example. "I am sad" states a feeling directly. "My heart is broken" expresses it metaphorically, but ordinary people use that expression too. Metaphor cannot be the private property of poetry. Advertising, jokes and political speeches also use repetition, imagery and ambiguity.

Analogy. Everyday cooking and a carefully prepared feast use many of the same ingredients. The arrangement, concentration and purpose differ. Shared ingredients do not mean identical meals.

Literariness means qualities that invite literary attention: patterns, suggestive meanings, unusual wording and effects on the reader. Carter discusses a gradient or cline of literariness. Falvey and Kennedy's Introduction also supports a continuum; Wasiuzzaman reviews this debate in Section 2.1 of his article. Neither overlap nor a continuum means that all texts have the same purpose or effect.

Features worth understanding

Foregrounding makes a feature stand out, encouraging the reader to notice it. It can arise through deviation or repeated patterns. Deviation departs from an expected pattern: "The silence screamed" gives silence an impossible action. Parallelism repeats a structure: "I waited at dawn. I waited at noon. I waited at night." The pattern emphasizes duration and frustration.

Imagery evokes sensory experience, such as "the rough, cold stone." Ambiguity allows more than one interpretation. Register is language associated with a situation or activity, such as legal or conversational English. Style is the characteristic way language is used. These features occur outside literature as well.

In teaching, compare an unusual phrase with a more usual one, explain the difference in effect, and let learners create another example. Lazar's poetry chapter recommends linking unusual language to learners' knowledge of normal language patterns. Do not teach poetic deviation as the standard pattern for every situation.

Why the definition of language matters

If language means only correct grammatical sentences, literature may appear to be a collection of rule-breaking oddities. If language includes making meaning in social contexts, expressing feelings and interpreting implied meanings, literature becomes highly relevant. A useful ELT view includes form, function, context and interpretation.

Referential language mainly communicates information: "The train leaves at eight." Representational language invites an imaginative response: "The train carried her last hope away." These are useful orientations, not sealed categories. A poem can give information; an advertisement can invite imagination. Daskalovska and Dimova argue that literature expands learning beyond predominantly informational classroom communication.

4 Why literature can help and why it can fail

Collie and Slater's four reasons

Remember A C L P: authentic material, cultural enrichment, language enrichment and personal involvement. This is a memory aid for their four headings in Chapter 1, pages 3 to 6.

Authentic material. Most literary works were written to communicate with readers, rather than to practise an ELT structure. Learners meet genuine language uses, including narration, argument and irony. An original poem is authentic in this sense, but it may still be unsuitable for a beginner. A graded reader can be appropriate although it has been adapted for learning.

Cultural enrichment. A story shows relationships, values, customs and social expectations in a vivid setting. An Austen passage can reveal connections among class, money and marriage. However, one fictional family does not represent a whole country. Lazar emphasizes critical treatment of cultural representations.

Language enrichment. Context reveals how vocabulary, grammar and discourse work together. Learners meet collocations, cohesion, sentence patterns and connotation. A story also creates reasons to speak and write. The word whispered becomes meaningful when a character fears being heard.

Personal involvement. Learners care about a character or question and want to continue. English becomes the means of discovering what happens. This involvement can sustain attention and make encounters with language memorable, although no text interests every learner.

Lazar adds related benefits: motivation, interpretative ability, language awareness and expression of opinions and feelings (Chapter 1, especially pages 14 to 19). Hoque supports integration of language and literature and contextual vocabulary work. Rahayu illustrates how a story can combine linguistic input with discussion of a human problem.

How context makes words memorable

Consider hope as an isolated definition. Then consider Johnsy in O. Henry's The Last Leaf: she expects to die when the final leaf falls, but the apparently surviving leaf gives her renewed hope. The word now belongs to an event, feeling and consequence. Learners can recall the story when recalling the word.

Context supplies clues, emotional associations and links among words. Follow-up tasks require retrieval and reuse. This explains a possible route to durable learning; reading a story once does not guarantee permanent vocabulary acquisition. Collie and Slater discuss memorable contextualized language on page 5; Lazar discusses meaningful and memorable contexts on page 17.

Judgement. Literature is a valuable resource when selection and teaching are appropriate. It should complement other materials needed for everyday, academic or professional communication. Its benefits are possibilities created by good teaching, not automatic properties of every famous text.

5 Lazar's three approaches

An approach is a general view of how learning should happen. A method is a more organized teaching system. A technique is a particular classroom procedure, such as jumbled lines or prediction. Do not confuse choosing a poem with choosing an approach.

ApproachMain concernExample using The Last LeafMain risk
Language-based approachHow language produces meaning and provides practiceInfer pneumonia and compare past-tense actionsMechanical analysis without response
Literature as contentLiterature itself, its context and conventionsStudy O. Henry's narrative technique and endingToo much lecture or difficult criticism
Literature for personal enrichmentLearners' experiences, feelings and opinionsDiscuss how hope influences actionDemanding a response without language support

Lazar presents these approaches in Chapter 2, pages 23 to 27. They can be combined. First understand the language, add enough context, and invite a personal response.

What a language-based approach actually does

It examines wording, grammar and discourse to help learners interpret the text or practise English. Students infer meanings, reorder events, complete gaps, compare expressions and justify interpretations. Stylistics is the close study of linguistic choices and their effects. It is a possible part of a language-based approach, not a synonym for every language exercise.

Example. "She entered" and "She crept in" describe similar movement, but crept suggests secrecy or caution. Ask what supports that inference in the passage. Learners link a word choice to characterization.

Analogy. Stylistics is examining how a musician's choices create the sound, rather than merely naming the instrument. Naming a metaphor is a starting point; explaining its effect is the real work.

A balanced combination. For a story about a parent's sacrifice, identify key expressions, give the necessary social background, then discuss whether the decision was justified. The teacher provides tools and evidence without prescribing every response. Collie and Slater also favour varied, student-centred activities rather than continuous explanation (Chapter 1, pages 7 to 10).

6 Selecting and adapting literary texts

Course learner and text

Lazar organizes selection around the course, the learners and the text (Chapter 3, pages 48 to 56). Start with a particular group and purpose. "This poem is famous" is not enough.

Course. What is the English level? What skills are needed? Is the course general English, academic English or another type? How much time is available? Does the syllabus require a genre or text?

Learners. Consider age, intellectual and emotional maturity, interests, cultural background, linguistic proficiency and literary experience. An adult beginner may need simple English with mature content, rather than a childish story. Literary and language proficiency do not necessarily develop together.

Text. Check linguistic difficulty, cultural demands, length, availability, relevance, literary interest and exploitability. Can you design useful tasks? Will essential meanings remain accessible? Does an extract supply enough context to stand meaningfully on its own?

Analogy. Choosing a text is like choosing shoes for a journey: beauty matters, but fit and purpose determine whether the traveller can walk.

Text difficulty and task difficulty

A simple text can support a demanding task; a difficult text can support a simple task. With a short poem, identifying repeated words is easier than defending two interpretations. With an unfamiliar extract, finding who speaks may be manageable even when full interpretation is not.

The supplied Literature in ELT handout discusses this distinction through Duff and Maley. Adjust tasks as well as texts. Adaptation may simplify wording, shorten a passage or add a glossary. Preserve enough context, tone and artistic effect for the planned lesson. Wasiuzzaman's discussion shows that over-shortening can damage usability.

Authenticity has more than one meaning

Textual authenticity concerns a text's genuine communicative origin. Perceptual authenticity, as used in Wasiuzzaman's framework, concerns whether learners can recognize and meaningfully respond to the represented world, with reasonable support. A genuine British text can have low accessibility for a particular group. An unfamiliar setting can still connect through recognizable themes such as friendship or family conflict.

Falvey and Kennedy's Introduction questions a narrow idea of authenticity that values documents simply because they are used outside class. Interest, recognition and a meaningful learner response also matter.

7 Wasiuzzaman's evaluation and its exam uses

Purpose and method

Md. Wasiuzzaman's An Evaluation of the Literary Materials used in the English Textbook for Class Nine Students in Bangladesh evaluates literary materials in the textbook introduced with the 2024 curriculum changes. This is an evaluation of that textbook, not a claim about the curriculum currently in force.

The research question asks about the materials' strengths and weaknesses. The study uses a qualitative design and document analysis, applying twelve theory-based questions, mainly adapted from Lazar. It does not report an experiment measuring students' language gains. Its findings concern suitability and teaching potential (article Sections 3 to 5).

The twelve questions in easy language

1. Does the textbook include different literary genres?

2. Can learners recognize and relate to the world of the texts?

3. Is the language and style challenging at a suitable level?

4. Does it consider learners' previous literary experience?

5. Does it include non-native literature in English?

6. Is the length manageable, with suitable adaptation where needed?

7. Are glossaries, vocabulary work and other language supports adequate?

8. Is enough cultural, historical and literary background supplied?

9. Are learners encouraged to connect the texts with their own lives?

10. Are the task instructions clear?

11. Do layout and visuals support understanding?

12. Is there sufficient guidance for teachers?

Questions 1 to 6 concern the selection and fit of texts; questions 7 to 12 largely concern the help and activities around them. Remember that text quality and teaching support must be evaluated together.

Findings you can actually use

Strengths. The book represents poetry, fiction and drama. Most materials consider learners' backgrounds reasonably well. The glossary provides English and Bangla meanings, examples and space for students' own sentences. Instructions are generally clear, visuals support texts, and a teacher guide offers lesson guidance.

Weaknesses. Crossing the Bar makes demanding thematic and cultural assumptions. The Pride and Prejudice extract is linguistically and conceptually challenging. The novel extracts are too short to supply adequate context, especially when teaching tone in Charlotte's Web. No published literary piece by a non-native English author is included. Background information and links to learners' own lives could be more frequent. The adapted Macbeth is broadly suitable but occupies substantial space.

Overall judgement. The article concludes that strengths outweigh weaknesses, while recommending better selection, contextualization, representation and learner connection. Do not describe its conclusion as a wholesale rejection of the textbook.

Three memorable teaching examples

The Last Leaf. Personifying pneumonia as a cold visitor makes a disease concrete and gives language learners material for imagery and vocabulary. The article considers the story broadly appropriate.

Charlotte's Web. Sentences can be accessible, yet tone remains hard to interpret when learners do not know who speaks to whom or why. This shows that sentence difficulty and contextual adequacy are different criteria.

Macbeth. The adapted scene can teach persuasion, dialogue and figurative language. Adaptation helps accessibility while retaining some characteristic effects. A suitable text still needs tasks and background support.

Exam sentence. "Wasiuzzaman's document analysis shows that literary materials should be evaluated not only for their language level but also for context, learner relevance and the guidance surrounding them." Then give one concrete finding. This is stronger than dropping the teacher's name without explaining the evidence.

8 Culture non-native literature and Bangladesh

Culture-sensitive teaching

Culture-sensitive teaching considers how learners' backgrounds and the values within a text affect understanding. A request that appears polite in one setting may sound distant or unusual in another. Literature shows such language within relationships and situations.

Example. When reading an Austen extract, discuss how wealth and social position affect characters' choices, then compare a specific situation with learners' experience. Do not infer that all British people behave like the characters.

Analogy. A novel is a window into one room of a society, not an aerial photograph of the whole country. Lazar's Chapter 1 and Chapter 4 support contextual help and critical cultural interpretation.

Literature offers emotions, viewpoints and social detail that can deepen cultural learning. However, films, interviews and ordinary interactions also teach culture. The claim that no culture-sensitive teaching can be effective without literature is too absolute.

Non-native literature in English

The past papers use this term for English-language literature emerging from settings outside the traditional native-English centres. It need not mean literature translated into English. A translated Bangla story can be useful, but translation and original English writing should be distinguished.

Benefits. Familiar cultural experiences can reduce the background burden; learners see English used to express local identity; the syllabus represents a wider range of Englishes; comparisons support intercultural awareness. Wasiuzzaman draws on Kachru in Section 3.5 and notes the absence of non-native English authors in the evaluated textbook.

Limitations. Local origin does not guarantee easy language, familiar themes or good teaching potential. Explain regional expressions and register. Do not reject British or American works simply because they are foreign. A balanced selection can combine local recognition with international exposure.

9 Poetry as a language resource

What poetry offers

Poetry can concentrate sound, vocabulary and meaning in a short space. Rhythm, stress, rhyme and intonation support listening and oral work. Imagery and metaphor invite interpretation. Repetition and memorable situations can support language practice. Not every poem rhymes, and not every poem has difficult language.

Example. Dickinson describes hope as a bird in the soul. Learners connect feathers, perches and sings with the bird image, then explain what this suggests about hope. They move from vocabulary to figurative meaning and discussion.

Analogy. A poem is like a small cup of strong tea: a little can contain a great deal. Its concentration can help a lesson, but learners may need help to enjoy it.

Problems. Unusual grammar, archaic vocabulary, cultural references and ambiguity can make a poem difficult. Recitation may embarrass some learners. A teacher's explanation can replace students' own response. Lazar's Chapter 6 discusses both linguistic distinctiveness and tasks for different proficiency levels; Wasiuzzaman illustrates thematic difficulty through Crossing the Bar.

A clear sequence of activities

Before reading. Introduce a recognizable theme, invite title predictions and explain only essential vocabulary. During reading. Give a first reading for overall meaning; then use selected tasks such as matching images, completing gaps, grouping vocabulary or reordering lines. Learners explain the clues behind their choices. After reading. Discuss a supported interpretation, read aloud with purposeful stress, rewrite as prose, or create a few lines using the same pattern.

Teach sound in relation to meaning. Different stress on "I never said he stole it" changes what the speaker emphasizes. Similarly, oral reading of a poem should explore the speaker's feeling and purpose, rather than merely reward volume or memorized pronunciation.

Selecting a poem for a particular group

Check age and maturity, interests, language level, literary experience, cultural accessibility, length and planned tasks. A short poem can still contain a demanding metaphor. Match the language purpose to the text: a sound-rich poem for pronunciation, an accessible image for connotation, or a repeated structure for guided writing. Offer support and check whether learners can respond, rather than assuming brevity guarantees ease.

Poetry recitation and listening assessment

Distinguish listening to someone recite from reciting oneself. The first can produce evidence of listening comprehension; the second mainly shows oral production and possibly memorization. Do not award a listening score simply because a learner recites well.

For listening assessment, read or play a suitable poem while students complete tasks: recognize words, sequence ideas, select the speaker's mood or explain emphasis. Set consistent delivery and scoring. A first play can test gist; a second can test detail. Students' background knowledge and poetic difficulty can distort the result. Poetry is useful for selected listening abilities, but varied speech samples are needed to assess general listening competence. This is an assessment application of the oral work discussed by Lazar, not a listening-test model attributed to her.

10 Short stories novels and vocabulary

Short stories

A short story offers characters, setting and a complete sequence of events within manageable reading time. Suspense produces prediction; the ending encourages discussion. Short stories can provide vocabulary in a memorable context. Collie and Slater discuss their practical advantages in Chapter 9; Lazar develops lesson planning and tasks in Chapter 5.

Example. In a story about a child hiding a broken cup, crept, whispered and trembled form a meaningful set. Learners identify the clues, infer the words and discuss the child's fear. A list alone would not show how the words work together.

Features to use. Plot supports sequencing and summaries; characters support descriptions and viewpoint writing; setting supports descriptive language. A narrator may know more or less than a character, so inference is also important.

How to teach vocabulary rather than just translate it

1. Select a small set of useful words or phrases tied to the lesson objective.

2. Let learners read enough surrounding context for the event to make sense.

3. Ask them to infer meaning and point to the clues.

4. Confirm the meaning with a brief gloss, picture or explanation.

5. Explore pronunciation, word form, collocation and relevant connotation.

6. Require reuse in a retelling, dialogue or a new sentence.

7. Revisit the items later through recall, rather than immediately displaying every answer.

Worked example. "He crept towards the door, hoping nobody would hear him." The clue nobody would hear suggests careful, quiet movement. Compare crept, rushed and strolled. Then ask learners to finish: "I crept into the room because ..." They show whether they understand the word in a new situation.

Limitations. Context may be too weak for accurate guessing; students may guess wrongly; a story may contain rare or dated words; a single encounter is insufficient. Vocabulary work can also interrupt enjoyment. Hoque emphasizes contextual guessing; Rahayu's Button, Button lesson examines words and phrases before moving to learners' own production. Use context plus checking and repeated practice.

Novels

Novels provide sustained exposure and a richer development of characters, settings and language. They are especially useful for extensive reading, inference, summaries, cohesion and discussion. However, their length can overwhelm learners. Lazar's Section 5.7 recommends planning home and classroom reading and anticipating vocabulary and motivation problems.

A workable route. Choose a manageable novel or graded version; give a brief introduction; set realistic reading portions; use chapter logs or character maps; discuss key episodes in class; finish with a review, alternative ending or comparison of viewpoints. Support extracts with the situation, speakers and preceding events.

Analogy. A short story is a single visit; a novel is a longer stay. The longer stay offers more familiarity, but requires more time and stamina. Do not assume that dividing all chapters among groups means every learner has read the whole novel.

11 Drama and language in interaction

Why drama interests learners

Drama presents people pursuing goals through language: asking, refusing, persuading, threatening and apologizing. Conflict and suspense give these actions a purpose. Performance adds voice, gesture, movement, silence and relationships. Drama is therefore more than dialogue printed on a page.

Example. A character says "Fine." With a relaxed smile, this may signal agreement; with a pause and tense voice, it may signal resentment. Learners perform both versions and explain what changes. They practise intonation and interpret the relationship.

Analogy. A script is a musical score; performance makes its possibilities audible. The words remain important, but delivery affects meaning.

Lazar's Chapter 7 treats language and performance together. In her example from The Lower Depths, gaps in dialogue and questions about requests, repetition and intonation connect language choices with character relationships. Falvey and Kennedy's sourcebook includes both classroom drama and participatory theatre activities.

Choosing a drama scene

Select a short, coherent scene with manageable language, a clear situation, understandable relationships and useful communicative functions. Check the number of roles, cultural background, emotional demands, preparation time and available resources. An extract should have a meaningful beginning and end, with a short explanation of essential preceding events. Avoid requiring elaborate costumes or theatrical skill when the goal is language practice.

Practical classroom tasks

Before. Introduce who speaks, where they are and what each wants. Predict a response. During. Listen for gist; fill missing turns; identify requests and refusals; compare alternative ways of saying a line; rehearse in pairs. After. Perform, discuss the choices, change the relationship or setting, improvise the next turn, or write a scene review.

Scripted rehearsal supports pronunciation and confidence. Improvisation adds less predictable communication. Neither a memorized performance nor an attractive costume automatically demonstrates spontaneous speaking ability. Include feedback on intelligibility, appropriate expressions and interaction.

12 A complete integrated lesson plan

Class purpose and materials

Learners: an illustrative class of intermediate secondary learners. Time: 60 minutes. Theme: hope and support. Texts: a short teacher-written plot summary of O. Henry's The Last Leaf and the first stanza of Dickinson's poem on hope. For a fuller story lesson, provide a manageable adapted story beforehand. The summary below is a classroom paraphrase, not a quotation from O. Henry.

Objectives. By the end, learners should be able to explain the main idea of both texts, infer and use four target words, distinguish past actions from present descriptions, and produce a brief spoken and written response using the target language.

Target language: hope, recover, leaf, perch; simple past for completed story events; simple present for the poem's description. Teach the irregular form leaf/leaves. Use perch in an everyday example to check transfer: "The bird perches on the wall."

The classroom texts

Story summary. Johnsy became seriously ill. She watched the leaves falling from an ivy vine and believed she would die when the last leaf fell. Her friend Sue tried to encourage her. After a storm, one leaf seemed to remain. Johnsy regained hope and began to recover. Later, Sue explained that their neighbour Behrman had painted the leaf during the storm. He had fallen ill and died. His painted leaf had helped Johnsy want to live.

Poem excerpt by Emily Dickinson.

"Hope" is the thing with feathers —
That perches in the soul —
And sings the tune without the words —
And never stops — at all —

Why integrate the texts? The story presents hope through events; the poem presents it through an image. Their shared theme creates a meaningful connection, while their different forms provide a useful comparison. Listening supplies input; reading confirms understanding; discussion prepares writing. Each skill supports the next rather than being added merely to tick four boxes.

Support and assessment. Give a short glossary where needed, sentence starters for discussion, and an optional extension task. Check gist answers, sequencing, vocabulary use and exit sentences. Allow alternative interpretations supported by the texts. A sensible rubric for the written task considers meaning, target-word use and tense choice separately.

Textbook connection. Lazar's Chapters 5 and 6 guide staged tasks; Collie and Slater support varied learner participation; Wasiuzzaman highlights contextual information, language guidance and personal connection. The plan applies these principles; it is not copied from any author's sample lesson.

13 Literature CLT and postmethod pedagogy

Literature and communicative language teaching

CLT seeks meaningful, appropriate communication. Literature supplies content learners can predict, debate and respond to. A group deciding whether a character acted wisely must explain reasons and respond to others. This can develop grammar, vocabulary, discourse, social appropriateness and strategies for handling misunderstanding.

Daskalovska and Dimova argue that literature provides imaginative and expressive uses often neglected by predominantly referential materials. Collie and Slater favour activities that involve learners, while Lazar connects texts with personal response and interaction.

Judge the activity, not just its label. Is there a real purpose? Do learners exchange ideas? Can they choose how to express themselves? Do they listen and respond? Is language support sufficient? Do the outcomes suit this group? A teacher explaining a poem throughout the lesson provides input, but limited communicative practice.

Example. Give two groups different possible endings, then ask them to agree on the more convincing one using textual evidence. The groups have a reason to exchange information and defend choices. Include language feedback after discussion.

The postmethod question

Postmethod pedagogy asks teachers to design informed teaching for their situation instead of assuming one universal method. Kumaravadivelu's three parameters are particularity (attention to local learners and context), practicality (teachers developing and examining a theory of practice) and possibility (attention to identity and social conditions). Source: Kumaravadivelu (2001), Toward a Postmethod Pedagogy, https://doi.org/10.2307/3588427.

Application to literature. Choose relevant texts; adapt and reflect on classroom activities; use characters and situations to explore perspectives and identity. These are applications of the framework. Adding a literature course alone does not meet all postmethod concerns. Teacher judgement, learner participation and institutional conditions still matter.

Analogy. Literature supplies ingredients. The teacher still has to plan, cook and taste the lesson.

14 Ronald Carter from the actual essay

His main argument

Carter's essay Linguistic Models, Language, and Literariness: Study strategies in the teaching of literature to foreign students supports integrated language and literature work. Language activities offer a way into a text and can develop sensitivity to its organization. Understanding language alone is not the whole of literary understanding. His strategies are preliminary or pre-literary, not a replacement for every other kind of literary study.

He illustrates them through Maugham's The Man with the Scar, a story with two narrators, a violent past and an unexpected explanation of the visible scar. Students examine what is told, how it is told, and what is left unstated.

The five named teaching strategies

1 Prediction. Stop a plot-driven story at a key point and ask what will happen. Students justify guesses from earlier clues. This sustains attention and produces discussion. Carter warns against overusing it; a descriptive lyric poem may not benefit from plot prediction.

2 Cloze procedure. Delete selected words and ask learners to propose or choose replacements. They examine immediate wording and the whole story. Several words may be defensible; ask why each fits and what effect it creates. A word list or first letter can provide support.

3 Summary. Ask for a short account of events, often with a word limit. Learners select information and reshape sentences. Compare summaries to see how selection changes emphasis. Carter cautions that a summary is an enabling activity, not the complete meaning or value of a story.

4 Forum. Organize debate around opposing interpretations. Groups defend views through text and relevant experience, listen to challenges and reply. "Forum" is Carter's practical label for discussion, not a separate technical theory. A range of interpretations still requires evidence.

5 Guided rewriting. Change the presentation of information or the discourse style. Add missing background to an opening or compare it with a newspaper report. Learners explore audience, purpose, style and how information affects readers. Rewriting is purposeful transformation, not merely replacing difficult words with synonyms.

Carter uses the model to reveal patterns and omissions. In The Man with the Scar, limited explicit evaluation and an unusual return to the scar invite questions about meaning and narrators' attitudes. Comparing stories, anecdotes and news reports can develop discourse awareness. Models should be tested and adjusted against texts, not treated as fixed rules. The teacher chooses how explicitly to introduce them, according to learners and purposes.

Remember. Five strategies provide entry; narrative analysis deepens interpretation; both remain learner-centred and evidence-based. A model helps students ask better questions, not just label six boxes.

Answer 1 Literary language and the definition of language

Literary English is not a separate language with its own exclusive grammar. Literature uses the same resources as everyday communication, although it may arrange them with unusual density and artistic purpose. Metaphor, repetition and ambiguity also appear in ordinary conversation, advertising and jokes. Saying "My heart is broken" therefore challenges a strict literary/non-literary division.

Carter describes a cline of literariness, and Falvey and Kennedy support a continuum. Lazar shows how unusual language can develop awareness of ordinary patterns. Nevertheless, overlap does not erase differences in purpose or effect. A poem may foreground sound or invite several interpretations more strongly than a timetable.

The definition of language shapes teaching. A grammar-only view makes poetic deviation appear irrelevant. A broader view includes contextual meaning, expression and interpretation. Teachers can compare ordinary and unusual expressions, explore their effects and ask learners to create examples. Literature then supports language learning without becoming a model for every everyday utterance.

Answer 2 Reasons for literature and the debate

Literature can provide meaningful English learning, but its value depends on the learners and activities. Collie and Slater identify authentic material, cultural enrichment, language enrichment and personal involvement. Lazar adds motivation, interpretative ability and language awareness. A story can teach words and structures while giving learners something worth discussing.

For example, The Last Leaf connects vocabulary about illness and hope with a memorable human situation. Learners can infer words, retell events and discuss the characters' choices. These tasks connect language with meaning.

Objections include difficult vocabulary, cultural distance, length and expressions unsuitable for everyday use. Excessive explanation may leave learners little opportunity to communicate. Suitable selection, limited glossing, background information and staged tasks can address many difficulties. Wasiuzzaman's evaluation illustrates both strong language support and weak contextualization in some materials. Literature should therefore complement other resources, with its use judged by learning objectives rather than literary prestige alone.

Answer 3 Literature as a powerful learning context

Literature can make vocabulary and structures memorable because they belong to meaningful events, relationships and feelings. Collie and Slater emphasize contextualized language enrichment; Lazar explains how striking contexts can support processing and acquisition. Learners remember a word with the situation in which it mattered.

In The Last Leaf, the apparently surviving leaf changes Johnsy's attitude. Vocabulary such as hope and recover becomes connected with consequences rather than an isolated definition. Dickinson's image of hope as a bird similarly connects an abstract idea with concrete words such as feathers and perches. Learners can explain and reuse these associations.

The teacher should move from understanding to retrieval and production: infer the word, confirm it, retell the event, then use it in a new context. Repeated encounters also matter. Literature offers conditions for lasting learning, but one reading cannot guarantee permanence. Difficulty, weak tasks or missing context can prevent the potential benefit.

Answer 4 Criteria for selecting literary texts

Selection should begin with a particular class and language objective. Lazar groups relevant considerations around the course, the learners and the text. Course factors include level, language needs, syllabus and time. Learner factors include age, interests, maturity, cultural background and literary experience. Text factors include difficulty, length, availability, relevance and exploitability.

A suitable short story might support contextual vocabulary, predictions and a brief written response. An extract should include enough information to understand the situation. Fame alone does not establish suitability.

Wasiuzzaman extends this discussion through a twelve-question evaluation framework. He examines genre variety, perceptual authenticity, suitable challenge, language and background guidance, clear instructions and teacher support. His criticism of short novel extracts shows why manageable length must not destroy context. Teachers should assess text difficulty and task difficulty separately, adapt support where necessary and review learners' responses. Good selection creates an achievable challenge with meaningful learning opportunities.

Answer 5 Non-native literature in English

Non-native literature in English can express experiences beyond the traditional native-English centres. For learners in Bangladesh, culturally recognizable settings may reduce the background burden and show that English can communicate local experiences and identity. Such texts also widen the range of authors and varieties represented in the syllabus.

Wasiuzzaman, drawing on Kachru, discusses English being contextualized within non-native cultures. He finds that the evaluated Class Nine textbook includes no published literary piece by a non-native English author and regards inclusion as a possible improvement.

Teachers can compare how two texts represent a familiar theme and how their language reflects different settings. However, local authorship does not guarantee simple English or an accessible topic. Original writing in English should also be distinguished from translation. The same criteria of relevance, difficulty and exploitability apply. A balanced course can combine familiar cultural ground with exposure to other societies, rather than making either local or foreign literature the only resource.

Answer 6 Lazar's three approaches

Lazar identifies a language-based approach, literature as content, and literature for personal enrichment. The language-based approach examines language choices or uses the text for English practice. In The Last Leaf, students might infer vocabulary, identify past-tense events and explain personification. Its risk is mechanical analysis without meaningful response.

Literature as content studies literature itself: authors, movements, genres, historical background and artistic effects. It can deepen understanding, but may become difficult or teacher-dominated. Personal enrichment connects a text with learners' feelings, experiences and opinions. Discussing whether a character's sacrifice was justified can produce meaningful communication; however, learners need linguistic support and should not be forced to disclose private experiences.

These approaches can work together. A lesson can clarify key language, supply necessary context and invite a supported response. The appropriate balance depends on the class and objectives. Integration preserves both language learning and the literary experience, while avoiding reliance on one approach for every learner.

Answer 7 Arguments for and against poetry

Poetry offers a compact resource for sound, vocabulary and interpretation. Rhythm, stress and intonation support oral work; images and figurative meanings encourage language awareness; repeated patterns can stimulate writing. Lazar's poetry chapter provides activities for different levels, showing that poetry is not restricted to advanced learners.

For example, Dickinson's bird image enables students to connect literal vocabulary with the idea of hope. They can identify the image, discuss its effect, read the lines aloud and create a similar comparison.

However, unusual syntax, archaic expressions, cultural references and ambiguity can confuse learners. A short poem may make large interpretative demands. Wasiuzzaman's discussion of Crossing the Bar illustrates thematic difficulty. Teachers should choose an appropriate poem, supply essential support and move gradually from comprehension to response. The aim is neither to avoid all challenge nor to explain everything. Poetry is useful when text and tasks suit the learners' language needs and capacity to respond.

Answer 8 Assessing a poem's suitability

A poem should be evaluated for a specific group and purpose. Following Lazar, consider the learners' proficiency, age, interests, maturity, literary experience and cultural background, together with course time and objectives. Examine vocabulary, syntax, imagery, allusions and the amount of support needed.

Next consider exploitability. A poem with clear repeated structures may support grammar and guided writing; sound patterns may support pronunciation; an accessible metaphor may support connotation and discussion. Shortness alone does not establish suitability.

Wasiuzzaman finds that several textbook poems connect with learners' experience, while Crossing the Bar makes demanding thematic assumptions. This supports assessing form and content separately. A linguistically easy poem can still be conceptually difficult. Teachers can vary task difficulty, pre-teach essential items and supply brief context. Learners' actual responses should inform the final judgement. The best choice creates meaningful challenge and gives students a realistic opportunity to understand, discuss and use English.

Answer 9 Poetry recitation for listening assessment

Poetry recitation can contribute to listening assessment when learners listen and complete tasks that demonstrate comprehension. It may test recognition of sounds and words, attention to stress, understanding of gist and detail, or inference about mood. Lazar's oral poetry activities provide a teaching basis for such work.

A teacher can read a suitable poem and ask learners to choose its main idea, fill selected gaps or explain which word receives emphasis. Clear task instructions, consistent delivery and defined scoring make the evidence more interpretable.

However, reciting a memorized poem mainly assesses oral performance and memory, not listening comprehension. Poetic language and rhythm also differ from everyday conversation, and unfamiliar imagery may introduce difficulties unrelated to listening. Poetry should therefore be one component of assessment. Other samples, such as dialogue or spoken explanation, are needed for broader listening ability. The tool is valuable when the score is tied to the listening behaviour actually demonstrated.

Answer 10 Short-story vocabulary lesson tasks

A short story provides a coherent context for vocabulary learning. Lazar recommends staged work, while Hoque emphasizes contextual guessing. Before reading, introduce the theme and explain a few essential words. During reading, ask learners to understand the event, infer selected items and identify supporting clues. Confirm meaning before moving to word forms, collocations and relevant connotations.

For "He crept towards the door, hoping nobody would hear him," students infer careful, quiet movement. Comparing crept, rushed and strolled shows differences that a simple translation may miss. After reading, students reuse words in a retelling, a short dialogue and new sentences; later review checks retention.

Limitations include weak context, inaccurate guesses, rare words and excessive interruptions to reading. A single encounter may not establish productive knowledge. Select useful items and combine inference with checking, reuse and repeated encounters. The story should remain meaningful, so vocabulary work supports comprehension instead of dismantling the narrative.

Answer 11 Using novels in class

Novels provide sustained language exposure, contextualized vocabulary and developed characters. They support extensive reading, inference, cohesion and discussion. Lazar's Section 5.7 stresses planning reading volume, vocabulary support and interest; Collie and Slater's novel chapter demonstrates a range of activities.

Choose a manageable work and introduce its setting and main relationships. Set achievable home-reading portions. In class, use predictions, chapter summaries, event sequencing, character maps and discussion of key episodes. A character diary or alternative ending connects reading with writing; role-play and discussion add speaking and listening.

Length, difficult language and loss of motivation are important limitations. Graded versions or selected extracts can help, but extracts need sufficient context. Wasiuzzaman's criticism of short novel passages shows that understandable sentences alone may not explain tone or relationships. Teachers should check reading progress and vary tasks without testing every detail. A novel succeeds when the reading load and classroom use fit the learners, rather than exhausting them.

Answer 12 Principles for selecting a drama scene

A drama scene should suit the learners' language level, interests and communicative needs. Lazar's selection principles apply, together with performance considerations from her plays chapter. Choose a manageable scene with a clear situation, recognizable relationships, useful functions and a meaningful sequence of interaction.

For teaching requests and refusals, select exchanges that learners can compare and perform. Give necessary preceding context, discuss what each character wants and ask how wording or intonation changes the effect. Check the number of roles, preparation time, sensitive content and resource demands.

A short extract may still contain difficult irony or depend on missing events. Archaic language may need adaptation; elaborate staging can distract from the learning objective. Wasiuzzaman regards adapted Macbeth as broadly appropriate while raising a length concern. Good scene selection balances literary interest, accessibility and practical tasks. The final criterion is what this class can learn through the scene, not merely its importance in the play.

Answer 13 Elements of drama that interest learners

Drama interests learners through conflict, suspense, characterization and the visible goals of speakers. Dialogue brings language into action: a character persuades, refuses, apologizes or challenges. Performance adds intonation, gestures, movement, silence and relationships. Lazar treats both the language and performance of a play as sources of meaning.

For example, students can perform "Fine" as willing agreement and as reluctant acceptance, then explain the voice and gesture choices. They learn that communicative meaning depends on context as well as vocabulary. Gap-filling dialogue, rehearsed role-play and improvising a new turn can develop different aspects of interaction.

Participation can also build confidence and personal involvement, consistent with Collie and Slater's approach. However, acting anxiety, unequal roles and memorization may limit language practice. Offer rehearsal, small-group roles and alternatives to public performance. Feedback should address intelligibility, appropriate language and interaction. Dramatic interest becomes educationally useful when it produces purposeful language use.

Answer 14 An integrated story and poem lesson

An integrated lesson should join the texts through a meaningful theme and clear language objectives. Section 12 provides a 60-minute plan using The Last Leaf and Dickinson's poem on hope. Learners practise four vocabulary items, simple past for story events and simple present for the poem's description.

Begin with a discussion of encouragement and predictions. Learners listen to the story, read and sequence events, and infer vocabulary. They then listen to and read the poem, connect its bird image with hope, and compare tense choices. Pair discussion prepares a short written response. An exit task checks word meaning and tense use.

The story makes hope visible through actions; the poem expresses it through an image. Their shared theme gives the integration coherence. Lazar supports staged activities, Collie and Slater learner involvement, and Wasiuzzaman language guidance and contextualization. Supply glosses and sentence starters where needed. Assessment should check the stated objectives rather than reward only literary interpretation or recitation.

Answer 15 Bangladesh challenges and solutions

Teaching language through literature in Bangladesh requires attention to learners' proficiency, familiar experiences, classroom resources and examination demands. Difficult wording or cultural assumptions can obstruct understanding; large classes and limited time may reduce participation. Teachers should choose manageable texts, give essential support and design clear pair or group tasks.

Wasiuzzaman provides specific evidence from his textbook evaluation. He identifies weak contextualization in novel extracts, insufficient background in some materials and limited connections to learners' lives. He also recognizes strengths in glossaries, instructions, visuals and teacher guidance. These findings suggest improvement rather than abandonment of literature.

Teachers can explain the situation before reading, use familiar themes as entry points and connect literary activities with assessable vocabulary, grammar and skills. Including suitable non-native English literature can widen representation. Gradual inference tasks build confidence. These responses should be adapted to the actual class; the article is document analysis and does not prove that every Bangladesh classroom faces identical conditions.

Answer 16 Literature and CLT and approach suitability

Literature can serve CLT by giving learners meaningful content for communication. Prediction, disagreement about characters and comparison of endings create reasons to express ideas and respond to others. Daskalovska and Dimova emphasize the imaginative and expressive dimension that literature adds to predominantly referential materials.

For example, pairs can propose different endings and negotiate which is better supported by the story. They practise explaining, asking for clarification and justifying choices. Lazar's language-based and personal-enrichment approaches can support this work; Collie and Slater encourage varied student-centred activities.

Suitability should be judged by objectives, learner needs, available time, language support, participation and outcomes. Ask whether learners have a purpose, choices and opportunities to respond, rather than merely repeat an answer. Content study may supply useful background, but a long lecture offers limited interaction. Literature does not make a lesson communicative by its presence alone. Methodology determines whether the potential for communication is realized.

Answer 17 Literature and culture-sensitive teaching

Literature can enrich culture-sensitive language teaching because it places language within relationships, values and social situations. Collie and Slater identify cultural enrichment as a major benefit. Lazar encourages background support and critical interpretation, since a work presents a selective perspective rather than a complete society.

An Austen extract can show connections among class, money and marriage. Learners can examine what the passage reveals, compare a specific situation with their experience and question assumptions. Such work links linguistic meaning with cultural understanding. Wasiuzzaman's concern about contextual information demonstrates why this process needs support.

However, the claim that culture-sensitive teaching cannot be effective without literary texts is too strong. Interviews, films and everyday interactions can also supply cultural evidence. Literature offers distinctive imaginative access to experience, but should work alongside other resources. Its value depends on selection, explanation and comparison. The strongest judgement recognizes its contribution while avoiding stereotypes or presenting one fictional account as the whole truth about a culture.

Answer 18 Literature and postmethod pedagogy

Postmethod pedagogy values teaching informed by local context, teacher reflection and learners' social experience. Literature can contribute through relevant selections, adaptable activities and discussion of characters' perspectives. For instance, a teacher may select a locally meaningful story, try several tasks and revise them after observing learners' responses.

However, adding literature courses to a syllabus cannot by itself address all postmethod concerns. A literature class can still follow rigid procedures or centre on teacher explanation. Success depends on informed choice, meaningful participation and evaluation of what learners actually do. Literature is therefore a flexible resource within such pedagogy, rather than a complete solution. See Section 13 for the framework and its source.

Answer 19 Carter's language teaching strategies

Carter proposes integrating language and literature through preliminary, learner-centred activities. In his essay, illustrated through The Man with the Scar, prediction asks learners to anticipate events from clues; cloze focuses on missing words and their effects; summary develops selection and restructuring; forum organizes evidence-based debate; guided rewriting explores style and information presentation.

For example, learners can compare two summaries or rewrite an opening with fuller background, then explain how the reader's experience changes. These activities improve language while opening questions about narrative meaning.

Carter also applies Labov's model: abstract, orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution and coda. Patterns and omissions can reveal how a narrative guides interpretation. The model should be tested against the text rather than imposed rigidly. Carter warns that language understanding is not the whole of literary understanding, summaries do not exhaust meaning, and prediction does not suit every text. His integrated approach offers tools for investigation, supported interpretation and more articulate responses.

16 Every previous short-note topic

For a short note, give a clear definition, one example, its teaching value and one caution. The notes below cover all distinct short-note topics in the supplied papers. They also provide an analogy and a relevant source connection.

Metalanguage

Metalanguage is language used to discuss language or literature: clause, register, metaphor, narrator. Naming a metaphor helps students discuss its effect precisely. Like a mechanic's terms for car parts, it makes explanation easier. Features include precision, shared terminology and support for analysis. Lazar's Section 2.6 explains its role. Teach only useful terms; memorizing labels should not replace understanding a text.

Lesson planning

Lesson planning connects learner needs, objectives, materials, activities, timing and assessment. A story lesson may move from prediction to reading, discussion and writing. It is a route map showing where learning should lead. Features include staged support, alignment with objectives and checks on learning. Lazar's materials-design chapters provide the relevant basis. A flexible plan responds to the class rather than forcing every planned activity.

Style mixes

A style mix combines different styles or registers. "I hereby open the meeting. Right, lads, let's get moving" shifts from formal to casual language. Like changing clothes halfway through an event, the shift is noticeable and may be humorous. It can create contrast, characterization or irony. Lazar discusses mixed registers among unusual poetic features. Learners compare expressions and judge where each fits; a mixture is not automatically an error.

Figurative language in ELT

Figurative language communicates beyond a literal reading. "His heart is stone" suggests emotional hardness. It is a picture made with words, helping readers see an abstract quality. Metaphor, simile and personification can support vocabulary, inference and expressive writing. Lazar's poetry chapter and Wasiuzzaman's analysis of personification provide references. Explain the literal image and contextual effect; merely naming the figure is insufficient.

Word recognition skills

Word recognition means identifying written word forms efficiently, allowing attention to meaning. A learner quickly recognizing walked can concentrate on the event rather than decode each letter. It is like recognizing a familiar face without checking every feature. Repeated reading, noticing word families and matching forms with sounds can help. Hoque's vocabulary discussion supports contextual word work, but guessing meaning and word recognition are distinct processes; do not define them as identical.

Drama for teaching English dialogues

Drama places dialogue within speakers' relationships and purposes. A request followed by refusal can teach turn-taking, intonation and politeness. It is conversation with a visible situation around it. Learners complete missing turns, compare wording and perform exchanges. Lazar's Chapter 7 provides the source connection. Rehearsal supports confidence; adding a changed situation or improvised turn helps develop flexible interaction beyond memorization.

Layers of meaning of a word

A word has a basic referential meaning and can acquire associations, emotional force or symbolic meaning in context. Home names a dwelling but may suggest belonging; in another text it may suggest confinement. It resembles a house with several rooms of meaning. Features include denotation, connotation and contextual variation. Lazar's language-awareness discussion and the sourcebook's meaning-focused activities support teaching through comparison, not one permanent emotional meaning for every occurrence.

Intercultural communication

Intercultural communication is communication across differing cultural backgrounds. Learners may need to interpret unfamiliar conventions for requests, disagreement or respect. It is crossing a bridge while understanding both banks. Literature can support perspective taking, comparison and awareness of assumptions. Lazar's cross-cultural chapter is relevant. Discuss specific characters and situations; one literary scene does not establish how every member of a culture behaves.

Dos and donts in using literature

Do select for learner needs, give essential context, vary tasks and accept interpretations supported by evidence. Do not equate fame with suitability, explain every line for the whole lesson, or treat poetic usage as universally appropriate everyday English. The teacher is a guide who helps learners explore. Features of good practice are accessibility, participation and clear objectives. Collie and Slater, Lazar and Wasiuzzaman all provide support for these choices.

Literature for teaching connotative meaning

Connotation concerns associations beyond a word's basic denotation. Snake names an animal, but in a particular text it may suggest betrayal. Context supplies the colouring around the outline of meaning. Students compare a literal description with a figurative one and explain the evidence. Features include emotional associations, cultural variation and dependence on context. Lazar's figurative-meaning work is relevant. Connotations are not identical for all readers or situations.

Creative writing for learning English

Creative writing uses imagination to produce language: an alternative ending, a character's diary or new lines following a poem's pattern. A literary text is a springboard that supplies a situation and vocabulary. It supports purposeful reuse, personal expression and experimentation. Collie and Slater's activity repertoire, Lazar's post-reading tasks and Rahayu's prompts offer source connections. Give a clear task and appropriate support; limitless freedom can leave learners unable to begin.

18 Final revision and self check

Ten points to recall

1. Literary and everyday language overlap; their purposes and effects can differ.

2. Collie and Slater: authentic material, cultural enrichment, language enrichment, personal involvement.

3. Lazar: language-based approach, literature as content, personal enrichment.

4. Selection: course, learners, text; also assess the tasks and support.

5. Wasiuzzaman: twelve questions, qualitative document analysis, strengths outweigh weaknesses.

6. His key weaknesses: contextualization, difficult selections, representation and personal connection.

7. Poetry: sound and concentrated meaning; fiction: narrative context; drama: interaction and performance.

8. Plan learning through before, during and after stages, with measurable objectives.

9. Carter: prediction, cloze, summary, forum, guided rewriting; Labov adds narrative investigation.

10. Good interpretation uses evidence; good teaching checks what learners can understand and do.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not call every familiar metaphor a unique feature of literature. Do not claim authenticity guarantees suitability. Do not say every local text is easy. Do not treat literary competence as identical to language proficiency. Do not award a listening score merely for recitation. Do not describe Wasiuzzaman's evaluation as an experimental proof of learning gains. Do not call Labov's model Carter's own invention. Do not assume a model requires every narrative to contain six ordered stages.

Explain these aloud without looking

Why can literature be useful even when some language is unusual? How do the three approaches differ? What makes an authentic text unsuitable? What did Wasiuzzaman find about the novel extracts? How would you teach crept? Why combine the story and poem in the lesson plan? What distinguishes resolution from coda? How do Carter's five strategies produce learning? What makes a literary activity communicative? What evidence would you accept for listening comprehension?
